On the Discipline of Doing Less

Most productivity advice adds. The good stuff subtracts.

FOCUS MINIMALISM

Every few months I clean out my task list the way other people clean out a garage — not because I ran out of space, but because I can no longer tell what's load-bearing and what's just been sitting there since January. Most of it, it turns out, was never load-bearing. It was there because adding a line felt like progress, and removing one felt like admitting defeat.

That's the trick doing-less has to overcome: subtraction doesn't look like anything. Nobody screenshots an empty inbox and calls it a win, even though an empty inbox is often the result of harder decisions than a full one. Addition is visible. It photographs well. A new habit, a new tool, a new system — these are things you can point to. Restraint leaves no artifact behind, which is exactly why it's so easy to under-value and so hard to sustain.

The cost hides in the maintenance

What sold me on subtraction wasn't a productivity book. It was noticing that almost everything I'd added to my life in the name of improvement came with a maintenance tax I hadn't budgeted for. The app I adopted to organize my reading list needed organizing itself. The morning routine I built to save decision-making energy became, itself, eleven decisions. Each addition was small on its own. The sum of them was a second job I hadn't applied for.

Doing less isn't about ambition — I don't think wanting less is a virtue in itself. It's about noticing that most systems fail not because they lack a feature, but because they're carrying features nobody's using. The discipline isn't in resisting the new idea. It's in periodically asking what you could remove without anyone, including you, noticing it's gone. If the answer is nothing, you've built something honest. Usually the answer is not nothing.

The question I've started asking isn't "what should I add to make this better," but "what's already here that isn't earning its place."

A smaller test

I've started applying a small filter before I let anything new into a routine, a tool, or a page like this one: if I can't say specifically what it replaces, it doesn't go in. Not "what it adds" — what it replaces. Addition without subtraction is just accumulation wearing a productive outfit. This site, for what it's worth, went through four rounds of removal before it went through one round of writing. Most of what makes it feel calm isn't what's on the page. It's everything I decided didn't need to be.

None of this is a rule for everyone. Some people do their best thinking in the middle of more, not less — more tabs, more notes, more inputs feeding a genuinely generative kind of chaos. I'm not trying to convert anyone. I'm just noting, for my own future reference, that the version of my week I'm proudest of is almost never the fullest one. It's the one where I can tell you exactly why each thing on it is there.
